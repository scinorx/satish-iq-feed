Hello, and welcome to In Simple Terms with Satish.

A few days ago, I opened my Xfinity app and saw a prompt I was not expecting.

“Turn on WiFi Motion.”

I stopped.

My Xfinity gateway connects devices throughout my home to the internet.

It has no camera.

So how could it possibly detect someone moving inside my home?

Before pressing the button, I imagined a simple experiment.

Suppose I place my phone on the kitchen counter and activate the feature. The television is off across the living room, but it remains connected to the gateway in another part of the house.

For a moment, I stand still.

Nothing happens.

Then I walk across the room between the gateway and the television.

My phone lights up:

“Motion detected.”

I look at the television. Its screen is black.

I look at the router. There is no lens hiding on it.

What saw the person?

Nothing did.

The Wi-Fi signal felt the room change.

A radio signal does not travel along one perfectly straight line. It can pass through materials or reflect from walls, furniture, appliances, and people. Several versions may reach a device by different paths.

When a room is quiet, that complicated radio environment can form a relatively stable pattern.

When I cross the room, my body absorbs, reflects, and scatters some of that radio energy. The signal arriving at the television changes slightly.

The software does not need my picture. It looks for a disruption large and consistent enough to resemble movement.

Think about a dark swimming pool.

You cannot see what entered the water. But if ripples suddenly reach the edge, you know that something disturbed the pool.

Wi-Fi sensing reads the ripples.

Wi-Fi does not see the person. It measures what the person does to the signal.

Now imagine I repeat the experiment in another corner of the house—and receive no alert.

That is not necessarily a contradiction. Xfinity says its feature uses a compatible gateway and selected connected devices. Their placement creates the useful sensing areas. If strong Wi-Fi is not travelling through the space where I move, the system may have little to measure.

Then the family dog crosses the room and another alert appears.

The software noticed movement, but it did not understand the story.

Xfinity provides sensitivity controls and small-pet filtering. But without visual information, it cannot always distinguish a pet from a similarly sized child. Walls, furniture, weak signals, and other movement also change the pattern.

Xfinity also says WiFi Motion is not a professionally monitored home-security service and does not guarantee its performance.

An alert therefore means, “The radio environment changed enough to resemble motion.”

It does not mean, “A particular person entered,” or, “Your home is definitely safe.”

Engineers are developing Wi-Fi sensing for presence detection, smart buildings, and wellness. Research also explores gestures and breathing, but ordinary commercial routers should not be assumed to perform those tasks.

I return to the phone and open the motion activity feed.

There is no photograph of me crossing the room—only an event and a time.

One timestamp seems harmless. But a history of timestamps could reveal when a home becomes active, when movement normally stops, and when an apparently empty space changes.

If no picture exists, is this still observation?

It is less revealing than video, but it is still information about life inside a private place.

That is why consent, controls, retention, security, and honest product boundaries matter. Xfinity allows the feature to be turned off and says Comcast does not monitor the generated motion or notifications. Users must decide whether the benefit is worth enabling.

Return to the prompt in my app.

At first, it sounded as though my router had secretly grown eyes.

It had not. It was learning to notice disturbances in something already filling my home: radio waves.

For years, Wi-Fi had one obvious job—to carry information between devices.

Now the signal itself could become information about the space through which it travelled.

The question on my screen was no longer, “Can Wi-Fi detect movement?”

It could.

The more important question was:

“Do I want my internet connection to become a sensor?”
