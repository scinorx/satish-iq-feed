You are halfway through a chapter when your e-reader shows a low-battery warning.

A minute later, it shuts down.

But the screen does not become black glass like a powerless phone.

A message remains perfectly visible:

"Battery empty. Please charge."

Ten minutes later, the same message is still there.

If the battery is empty, what is holding that image?

The answer is e-paper, also called electronic paper.

Unlike an LCD or OLED screen, it does not keep rebuilding an image with light.

It physically rearranges the image—and then leaves it alone.

Imagine cutting through one black-and-white e-paper pixel and looking inside.

You would find tiny containers embedded in a thin film. In one common design, they are microcapsules roughly the width of a human hair. Inside each capsule is a clear fluid containing electrically charged white and black pigment particles.

Now place that film over a layer of microscopic electrodes.

When the display applies one electric field, the white particles move toward the visible surface while the black particles move away. That part of the screen looks white.

Reverse the field, and the black particles rise while the white particles move down. Now it looks black.

Across thousands or millions of controlled areas, those particles form letters, numbers, and pictures.

The important part comes next.

When the voltage stops, the particles do not immediately drift back to where they started. They remain in their new arrangement.

Engineers call this bistability: the pixel can remain in either of two stable states without continuous power.

The display remembers with matter, not light.

That is why your empty-battery message remains visible. It is also why an electronic grocery label can keep showing three dollars and forty-nine cents while an employee replaces its battery.

When fresh power arrives and the image changes, the device wakes its controller. The controller calculates which pixels must move and sends a carefully timed sequence of voltages, called a drive waveform, through the display's backplane.

You may see the screen briefly flash or retain a faint trace of the previous page. The controller is pushing pigment particles into controlled positions before drawing the new image. Better waveforms reduce this ghosting, but moving physical particles is still slower than switching light from an LCD or OLED pixel.

E-paper also looks different because it is reflective.

It uses the room light—or sunlight—that falls onto its surface and reflects it back toward your eyes. It does not need a backlight to make a page visible, so bright daylight can make it easier to read.

But in a completely dark room, the panel cannot glow by itself. An e-reader that works at night usually adds a front light above the e-paper layer.

Color versions either filter the black-and-white layer or move several colored pigments with precise electrical pulses. They usually trade faster updates and television-like brightness for a result that looks more like printed ink.

The same principle is now reaching larger signs. In 2026, LG announced a thirty-two-inch commercial e-paper display designed to draw power mainly when its content changes.

But here is the engineering catch.

Saying that e-paper needs no power to hold a static image does not mean the entire device uses no power.

Its processor, wireless radio, touch sensor, storage, front light, and display controller still need electricity. And every screen update consumes energy.

So e-paper is strongest when an image remains useful for minutes, hours, or days. A book page, room sign, menu, timetable, or price label fits that pattern.

Video does not.

You connect your e-reader to a charger.

The controller wakes, the screen flashes, and your book returns to the same chapter.

Then the electronics go back to sleep, while the pigment remains exactly where the last electric field placed it.

That is the simplest way to understand e-paper:

It is not a screen constantly shining a picture at you.

It is an electrically rearrangeable sheet of pigment that can hold a picture after the power stops.
